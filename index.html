More than a list of dates, history is the record of people and the choices that changed their world.

The United States emerged from Indigenous homelands and European colonization, became an independent republic, and grew through expansion, immigration, innovation, and struggle. Its history is still being written.

Native Nations & European Colonization

For thousands of years, Indigenous nations built diverse societies across North America. From the late fifteenth century onward, European arrivals brought colonization, trade, disease, and conflict, transforming lives and lands. Enslaved Africans were forcibly brought to the colonies, establishing a system of bondage that shaped the continent.

Many nations, many histories
Before 1607 – 1763

Revolution & the Founding

Disputes over representation and taxation led thirteen British colonies to declare independence in 1776. After victory in the Revolutionary War, the Constitution created a new federal government. The founding documents proclaimed liberty while slavery and unequal rights remained deeply embedded in society.

Independence & a new republic
1763 – 1800

Expansion & Disunion

The young nation expanded westward through purchases, migration, and war, often at the devastating expense of Native peoples. Intensifying conflict over slavery and its expansion fractured the country. The Civil War (1861–1865) preserved the Union; emancipation ended legal slavery, though freedom and equal protection remained fiercely contested.

Growth, removal & civil war
1800 – 1865

Reconstruction & Industrial Change

After the Civil War, Reconstruction amendments ended slavery and established citizenship and voting protections for Black men. White supremacist violence and discriminatory laws undermined these gains. Industrialization and railroads reshaped the economy, while immigration and urban growth transformed communities across the country.

Rebuilding & rapid transformation
1865 – 1914

World Wars & the Great Depression

The United States became a global power and entered both world wars. The 1929 stock market crash ushered in the Great Depression; President Franklin D. Roosevelt's New Deal expanded the federal government's role in relief and economic recovery. Wartime mobilization brought profound change—and continued injustice—at home.

Crisis, reform & global conflict
1914 – 1945

Cold War & Civil Rights

After 1945, rivalry with the Soviet Union shaped foreign policy, science, and everyday life. At home, the Civil Rights Movement challenged segregation and discrimination through organizing, legal action, and protest. Landmark laws in the 1960s advanced civil and voting rights, while movements for broader equality continued.

Rights movements & a changing world
1945 – 1991

A Connected, Changing Nation

The end of the Cold War ushered in a period of rapid globalization and technological change. The September 11, 2001 attacks reshaped national security and foreign policy. In the twenty-first century, debates over democracy, identity, economic opportunity, and the meaning of equality continue to influence American life.

An unfinished story
1991 – Today

A fuller picture

Whose story is told matters.

There is no single account that captures every American experience. Indigenous peoples, Black Americans, immigrants, women, workers, and many others have shaped the nation—often while confronting exclusion and injustice.

Understanding the past means looking at both the ideals people pursued and the realities they faced. It also means recognizing that historical interpretations can change as new evidence and voices come to light.
